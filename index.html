A  Plain answers
What is GYR-O Pages?

Every GYR-O page comes in two parts: Intro and Info. Intro, above the double line, is whatever this subject needs — its name, a picture, a paragraph — and it differs from site to site. Info, below the line, is the standard layout, the same on every GYR-O site: three columns of plain answers, sources and technical detail. A reader who has used one already knows where to look on the next.

A way of building a single-topic website so that one topic serves two readers at once: the newcomer who knows nothing about it, and the reader who already knows the subject well. Neither is watered down for the other, and neither has to wade through the other.

Its second idea is about footnotes. Instead of being buried at the foot of a page, tucked behind a link, or scattered wherever they fall, every reference is gathered into one column of its own — centered in the layout, given the same visual weight as the writing, and always in the same place.

They are three columns wherever they will fit as three columns. On a wide screen you see all three at once. On a phone they stack, and you read one at a time. Printed, they become one document. And where one column holds more than any column can — an entire municipal code, say — it is given its own page, as at worland-dog-law.online6. Stacked, paged or side by side, it is the same three. Usually it is a single page — no system to log into, no monthly bill, nothing that can be switched off.

Who is it for?

Anybody who has to be believed by strangers.

  • A small business whose customers want to know it is licensed, insured and real.
  • A tradesperson, inspector or consultant whose claims rest on standards and certifications.
  • A campaigner or citizen quoting a law, an ordinance, a filing.
  • A researcher publishing work that a skeptic should be able to check.

The common thread is not the subject. It is that a reader might reasonably ask says who? — and deserves an answer better than a bare link.

Why three columns rather than one long page?

Because one subject has to be met at more than one depth, and mixing the depths serves nobody. A customer scrolling past technical specifications stops reading. A specialist scrolling past an explanation of basic terms also stops reading.

  • Column A — the newcomer. What is this, what does it cost, why should I care.
  • Column B — the checker. Your sources, previewed, so they can verify without leaving.
  • Column C — the specialist. Full detail, methods and specifications.

Side by side, each picks a lane and stays in it — and can glance sideways when curious.

What is the middle column for?

Every source, previewed. When column A or C cites something, the citation is a small red numeral1 — the number you just passed. That is what they are called throughout this page. Clicking one moves you to a card in the middle that tells you what the source is, who made it, what you would find there, and what it is doing in this page.

For a business this column is the sales pitch. Not "we are licensed" but a card naming the license, the issuing authority, and a link to the register where anyone can check it in ten seconds. That is a different order of credibility from a badge image.

At the top of the column, before any source, one card names the most recent change to the page, so a returning reader sees at once what is new. And when a numeral has brought you to a card, a Back button at its foot takes you to the exact spot you left.

Why is the link the last thing in each card?

Because when it was first, readers clicked it immediately and never read the preview — which defeated the whole point. That was the single most important discovery in designing this.

By the time you reach the link you already know whether the trip is worth taking. Most readers stop at the preview, satisfied. That is a success, not a failure.

What happens when one of my links dies?

Links rot. Government pages move, companies fold, archives reorganize. A page that quietly turns into a row of dead ends is worse than no page.

So the links are checked2 and when one dies it is disabled, not deleted — you can still see where it pointed, the preview still says what was there, and a dated note appears at the foot of the middle column saying when the break was found.

Nothing vanishes. A reader years later can see exactly what you cited and that it was live when you cited it.

What does it cost — and what if the free hosting ends?

Free, without a name of your own. The hosting is free3, the security certificate is free4, and an address like yourname.github.io/site costs nothing and never lapses. A little more each year buys your own name instead — and that is the part you actually own, because a name you rent can be moved anywhere and a free address cannot.

Or share a name. A group can buy one name together — a farm cooperative, say, with washakiefarms.online — and every member’s site lives under it free, at an address like eastbench.washakiefarms.online, with one index page listing them all.

Think of license plates. Everyone gets one; a vanity plate costs a little extra each year, and it is up to you. A name of your own runs roughly $11 a year for a .com and up to about $28 a year for a .online once its cheap first year is over.

The honest answer to the second half. Nobody has promised free hosting forever, and you should distrust anyone who says otherwise. What protects you is not a promise — it is that your site is a handful of plain files. If the host ever changed its terms you could move the whole thing somewhere else in an afternoon, unchanged, and keep your address. There is nothing to export, nothing to convert, and nothing held hostage.

That is the real answer to lock-in, and it is stronger than "forever" because it does not depend on anyone's goodwill.

Is it secure?

Yes, and for a better reason than you would expect. It is not that the host has excellent security staff, though it does. It is that there is nothing there to attack.

No database, so nothing to inject. No login, so no password to steal. No forms and no server-side code, so nothing to run. No plugins, so nothing to go out of date. No cookies and no analytics, so no visitor data to lose. The entire site is text files being handed out.

The overwhelming majority of small-business sites that get hacked are running a content system with out-of-date add-ons. This has no content system and no add-ons. It cannot go out of date because there is nothing in it that has a version.

Will Google find it?

Half of that is automatic and half of it is you, and it is worth being clear which is which.

The technical half is as good as it gets by construction10. The page is small and loads almost instantly, works on a phone first, uses proper headings and structure rather than a soup of anonymous boxes, and has nothing blocking it from rendering. Those are the things a search engine measures and most sites struggle with.

The other half is your words and who links to you, and no format can supply that. Anybody promising you rankings is selling something.

Does it work on a phone?

It is built for the phone first. Below a certain width the three columns become three buttons and you read one at a time; above it, all three sit side by side. Same file, no separate mobile site, nothing to keep in step.

On a long page, once you have scrolled down, a round button with an up arrow appears at the bottom right. It takes you straight back to the top.

It also prints properly, which almost nothing does anymore: all three columns unfold into one document in order, with every collapsed answer opened.

Do I need GitHub?

Only if you want to make the changes yourself. GitHub is where the files sit, and where the page is served from. Somebody has to put them there once — you, or whoever builds the page for you. A page that goes up and is then left alone needs nothing from anybody after that.

If you do want to change your own page, you never have to see a command line. Two free things and about twenty minutes, once:

  1. A GitHub account3 — an email address and a password. Free.
  2. GitHub Desktop9, an ordinary application for Mac or Windows. Free. It puts a folder on your computer that is joined to the internet.

A change then goes live like this: edit the file, open GitHub Desktop, type one line saying what you changed, click Commit, then click Push. Live in about a minute.

Two words to learn. Commit means "save this version, with a note." Push means "send it to the internet." Everything else in that application can be ignored indefinitely. Column C has the setup written out step by step.

Does it take a team?

No. One person can write it, publish it and look after it, and that is more of an advantage than it sounds. Every pair of people on a job is one more conversation to keep going: two people have one, five have ten, ten have forty-five14. The count climbs like a hockey stick, and column C draws it. Each extra person also brings their own idea of how the thing should work, and every idea has to be squared with every other. One person has none of that — no meetings, no handoffs, no waiting on anybody else before a change can go live.

One is enough, not a limit. Every change is saved with a note saying what changed and who made it, so a second person can join at any time and see how the page got to be the way it is.

How do I change the words later?

Three ways, and you only need one of them.

  1. Ask whoever built it. For most subjects that is the whole answer, and it costs the builder a few minutes.
  2. Open the file and type over the words. The page is one file, and your sentences sit in plain sight between the tags that shape them. The rule is to leave the angle brackets alone and change what is between them.
  3. Ask an AI to make the change. Say what should be different, in plain English, and read what comes back before it goes up. That is how every site in this family is kept, including this one.

What this format gives up. WordPress15 exists to give you an editing box: sign in, type, click Update, and the page has changed. That box is the reason for the database behind it, the account you sign in to, the plugins that extend it and the updates that never stop — and for most of what goes wrong on small sites. There is no box here, because there is nothing to sign in to. What you have instead is a file anybody can open in fifty years.

What it asks of you. If you want changes made by asking for them in plain English, you need an AI assistant and enough familiarity with one to ask for what you want and to check what comes back. That is the one skill this format assumes — and teaching it is a main point of the pilot program this format came out of11: twelve sites, four workshops open to anyone, and a few local people given a light introduction to AI, so that a subject who wants a change has somebody nearby to ask.

Who sets one up?

One person, once. Making the repository, putting the files in it, turning the hosting on, buying the domain and pointing it at the host takes about twenty minutes the first time, and column C has it written out step by step. On the sites in this family a script does the domain end of it.

The person the site is for need do none of that. On the Washakie County pilot program11 a subject chooses a name and hands over the material — the ordinance, the price list, the record of work — and signs up for nothing, installs nothing and holds no password. The site is handed over at the end, domain and all.

Can I publish a running series rather than one page?

Yes, and this is where the format earns its keep for some trades. Each issue is dated, published the day it is written, and never revised afterward — superseded by a later issue if it turns out to be wrong, but never quietly edited.

A builder documenting a renovation, an inspector logging visits, a restorer recording a treatment, a researcher recording a day's tests5. What you end up with is not marketing. It is a dated, public, source-cited record of what was done and when, which is a materially different thing to own if anyone later disputes it.

Worth saying plainly: if that record matters to you legally, ask a lawyer how it would actually be treated. The format gives you the record; it cannot tell you what it is worth in a dispute.

How to read a card

Every card below reads the same way, top to bottom. Parts 4 and 5 are the ones a bare footnote never gives you.

  1. Number and kind — what sort of thing it is
  2. Title — what it is called
  3. Provenance — who made it, when, under what license
  4. Preview — what you would find if you went there
  5. Why it is cited — what it is doing on this page
  6. The link — last, and only last

C  How it works (Details)

Prior know-how is assumed here, and the items become more “technical” toward the bottom.

The shape

One HTML file and four small scripts. No framework, no build step, no dependencies except two web fonts, and those fall back to system faces if they fail to load. A typical page is 35–100 KB and has finished rendering before a loading spinner would have appeared. Words are drafted here first in plain text — CommonMark, the precisely specified form of Markdown7 — so they outlive whatever tool made them.

The scripts are phone-view.js, top-button.js, back-link.js and search.js. Each adds one control and what that control needs, nothing more, so a page missing one of them loses that control and still works. Wherever a site carries one, it is the identical file — changed once, in the template new sites are made from, and then copied out — so no copy drifts from the others.

Three columns wide, three buttons narrow, one document printed. The same markup serves all three — nothing is duplicated, because duplicated content is content that will drift.

Why the sources sit in the middle

Position carries meaning. A source column on the far right reads as an appendix; at the foot of the page it reads as an afterthought and goes unread. Between the plain answers and the technical detail it reads as what it is: the common evidence both other columns rest on.

It also means that whichever column you are in, a citation is one glance away rather than one long scroll.

And the card comes to you. Clicking a numeral does not send you off to find something. It brings the card up to where you are looking and marks it for a moment, so the eye lands in the right place instead of searching the column. The movement is not decoration: it is the difference between evidence arriving and evidence having to be hunted for.

Then it brings you back. A Back button appears at the foot of that card, and it returns you to the very numeral you clicked, outlined in gold for a moment, so that reading begins again at the word it stopped on. Both ends of the interruption are carried, not one: the trip out and the trip home are each a single click, and the thread the reader was holding is still in their hand.

That hunt is old. Printed notes at the foot of the page go back at least to 156816, and every one of them since has asked the same thing of a reader: leave the sentence, travel to the bottom of the page or the back of the book, find the mark again, come back if you can. Each trip costs a little of the thread the reader was holding. Four and a half centuries of that is long enough to have stopped noticing the cost.

Two blocks, always in the same place

Column B opens with two blocks built exactly alike: a heading, a caption line, then the content. Internal links, captioned The two most recent content changes., holds two cards, newest first — each a date and what changed, linked to the place on the page where it changed. Two rather than one because a single line reads as thin, and three begins to look like a log. External links, captioned Every reference to some other website., holds the source cards, one for every other site the page mentions.

The headings and captions are word for word the same on every site that carries them, and External always sits below Internal, in one column. A reader who has used one GYR-O site already knows where to look on the next. On a site in page form they head the page that is column B, as on worland-dog-law.online’s Ordinance Text page6, where External links is a plain list.

Citations and the way back

The six parts of a source card are listed at the top of column B, where a reader meets them. Items 4 and 5 do the work. Item 5 is the honest one: it forces the writer to say what the source actually supports, which is where padded citation lists fall apart.

Numerals — the small red numbers that follow a claim, each one a link to the card that supports it — belong in columns A and C, never in the header. A name in the header may still be a plain link.

Arriving at a card from a numeral adds one control at its foot, below the link: a Back button, pointing the way you came — ← Back to column A, Back → to column C. It returns you to the very numeral you clicked, outlines it in gold for a moment so the eye finds it, and then goes away. It is a way back, not something the card says; the link is still the last thing the card tells you.

Link health, done honestly

The obvious implementation is wrong. A browser cannot read the status of a cross-origin request: with mode:'no-cors' the response is opaque and status is 0 whether the page is healthy, missing or on fire2. An in-page checker built that way reports false deaths, which is worse than not checking at all.

So the check runs elsewhere, on a schedule, and writes a small file the page reads at load:

links.json
{ "checked": "2026-09-10",
  "dead":   { "r4": "2026-08-02" },
  "note":   "one source unreachable since 2 August 2026" }

The page fetches that file — same origin, so the status is readable — and for anything listed dead it disables the link, strikes it through, and writes the dated note at the foot of column B. If the file is missing or unreadable the page does nothing: no file means no claim, never a false alarm.

The principle, which generalizes. A feature that fails silently toward a false positive is worse than an absent feature. This page would rather say nothing about a link than say something untrue about it.

Security: why there is nothing to attack

The usual pitch is that the host has good security people. True, and beside the point. The reason this is hard to compromise is that the attack surface is close to empty:

no database          nothing to inject
no login             no credentials to steal
no forms             no input to poison
no server code       nothing to execute
no plugins           nothing to fall out of date
no cookies           no visitor data held
no analytics         no third party in the page

Nearly every small-business site that gets compromised is running a content system with an out-of-date add-on. There is no content system here and no add-ons. Nothing in the page has a version number, so nothing in it can become an old version.

What remains is genuine and worth naming: your account can be phished, and your domain registrar account can be taken over. Both are protected by two-factor authentication, and both are worth turning on before anything else.

Hosting, and what happens if the free tier ends

The pages are plain files served from a folder3, with the certificate issued automatically4. Nobody has promised that will be free forever and the format does not depend on it being so.

The protection against lock-in is structural rather than contractual: there is no database to export, no proprietary format to convert, no theme to rebuild. Copy the folder to any other static host, repoint the domain, and the site is identical. All internal links are relative, so a change of address touches one file.

Search: the half that is automatic

Everything a search engine asks of a page's construction10 is satisfied here without effort — one small page, no render-blocking scripts, mobile layout first, real headings, real landmarks, descriptive title and meta description, clean relative URLs, no interstitials.

What no format supplies is content worth finding and other sites linking to you. Any claim beyond the technical half should be treated as sales.

One caveat specific to this design: content hidden inside collapsed answers is in the markup and therefore indexable, but a reader arriving from a search lands on a closed accordion. Put the sentence that matters in the summary line, not only inside it.

Devices

A single breakpoint. Above it, three columns with the unselected two dimmed rather than hidden, so the whole page is scannable; a click anywhere in a dimmed column selects it, as its button would. Below it, one column at a time with the buttons acting as tabs. Nothing is a separate mobile site, so nothing can fall out of step. Phone view is for whoever builds the page, not whoever reads it. Its button stays hidden unless the address carries ?dev, and never shows on a screen that is already phone-sized; then it sits beside Download PDF and shows the page inside a phone-sized frame at the real widths of common phones and a small tablet, so the phone layout can be checked from a desktop. It stays on for the rest of that browser tab — the one thing the page ever stores, and only in the browser of whoever asked for it; ?dev=0 turns it off. A site that wants it for every reader marks its button data-public, as gyro-sites.online does.

Getting around: the buttons that come and go

Two buttons appear only when they are useful, and neither is printed. Scroll about a screen down and a round button with an up arrow appears at the bottom right. It takes you back to the top — smoothly, or at once if your system asks for reduced motion — and hands the keyboard to the page title, so the next Tab starts from the top. The page ends with a little blank space, so the last line of the footer can scroll clear of the button on a phone.

The other is the Back button on a source card, described above: it appears when a numeral sends you to column B, and goes away once it has brought you back. Both were first asked for on washakie-ai.online11 and then written into the format, so every new site starts with them.

Search, with the page as its own index

The box in the navigation bar searches this page and nothing else. When the page loads, search.js walks what is actually there — every answer in column A, every card in column B, every section in column C — and builds its index out of the text it finds. Nothing is kept by hand, so the index cannot fall out of step with the page: change a sentence and the next load indexes the sentence you wrote.

It earns its place here for a reason peculiar to this format: the answers in column A are folded shut, and a browser’s own find bar will not always look inside a folded answer. A reader could search for a word that is on the page and be told it is not. A result names the column and the heading it came from; choosing one opens that column, unfolds the answer if it is folded, and marks where you landed, the way a numeral does.

No index file, no service, nothing sent anywhere: it runs in the browser, over text the browser already has, and it stores nothing. With scripts off the box never appears, which is the honest state for a control that could not work.

Typography

Body text at 17 px, line height 1.62; on a wide screen each column holds about sixty characters to the line. Figure captions are set at 22 px — larger than body text, deliberately, because a caption is often the only thing a scanning reader reads; the caption under the picture at the head of this page is one. Three accent colors only: green for structure, gold for the column keys and for marking where you are, red for citations and for whatever a click has just brought you to. A link that has died turns gray and is struck through. Dark mode redefines the nine color properties, and one label color set outside them; the numerals keep their red on a white disc in both themes.

Print and PDF

The button calls the browser's own print dialog. Print rules unhide all three columns, drop the navigation, the floating buttons and the picture at the head of the page, and start each column on a fresh page; just before printing, a script opens every folded answer, and folds them again after — so a printed copy is a linear document in the order A, B, C, and its reader is missing nothing.

Accessibility

The buttons are a real tab set: role="tablist", aria-selected, panels labeled by their button, arrow-key navigation8. Answers are native <details> elements, so the browser's own find searches inside them. With JavaScript disabled entirely the page still reads: all three columns render, on a phone as on a desktop, and every answer still opens with a click. What is lost is whatever moves you around — the switching, the jump from a numeral to its card, the buttons, the striking-through of dead links — and printing then leaves the answers folded.

The Top and Back buttons carry spoken labels, since neither shows much text: Back to the top of the page, and Back to where this source is cited, in column A (or C).

Setting a site up, for someone who has used neither GitHub nor GitHub Desktop

Written for a reader who has never done any of it. No command line at any point.

Once, at the start — about twenty minutes

1  Make a free GitHub account.

2  Make a new repository, named for the site. Check "Public".
   A repository is a folder on their servers that keeps
   every version of everything in it.

3  Install GitHub Desktop and sign in.
   File > Clone repository > pick the one you just made,
   and choose where it sits on your computer. You now have
   an ordinary folder that is joined to GitHub.

4  Put index.html in that folder.

5  On github.com, in the repository: Settings > Pages.
   Source: main branch, root folder. Save.
   The site is live at username.github.io/reponame.

6  OPTIONAL, and the only step that costs money.
   Buy a domain. At the registrar, point it at GitHub:
     four A records on @  ->  185.199.108.153
                              185.199.109.153
                              185.199.110.153
                              185.199.111.153
     one CNAME on www     ->  username.github.io.
   Then add a one-line file called CNAME to the folder
   containing just your domain, and publish it.
   Set the DNS BEFORE publishing that file, or GitHub
   shows a red warning that clears itself once the
   records propagate.

7  Wait for the padlock. A few minutes after the domain
   resolves a certificate is issued automatically and a
   checkbox appears: "Enforce HTTPS". Check it.

8  Turn on two-factor authentication on both the GitHub
   account and the registrar account. These are the only
   two things anyone can actually take from you.

Sending a change live — about a minute

1  Edit index.html.
2  Open GitHub Desktop. Changes are listed on the left.
3  Type one line in Summary saying what changed.
4  Click "Commit to main".
5  Click "Push origin".
6  Live in about a minute. Add ?v= and today's date to the
   address when checking: the host caches for ten minutes
   and you will otherwise be looking at yesterday.

Two words, and no more. Commit is "save this version with a note." Push is "send it to the internet." Branches, pull requests and merges exist and can be ignored indefinitely.

Why this route rather than a website builder. Because every version is kept, dated and annotated, by default and without effort. For a standing page that is a convenience. For a running record it is the entire point.
Keep it small and keep it all. The slogan belongs to business rather than to software, and it is usually about money: keep the outfit small and the whole of what it earns stays inside it. The arithmetic is the same here, and what is kept is not only money. It is the decisions, one voice from the first line to the last, a record of every change and why it was made, and the hours a larger team would have spent keeping itself in step.

Editing: how a page changes after it goes up

There is no admin panel, and that is deliberate — an admin panel is a login, and a login is the one thing this format does not have. Page D is a form that writes text for you to paste into the file. It holds nothing, protects nothing, and can be thrown away.

So a change is made to the file, by one of three hands: the person who built the page, the owner in a text editor, or an AI asked in plain English. The third is how every site in this family is kept, and it is the one to be clear-eyed about. What it asks is not programming; it is the ability to say what should be different and to read what comes back. What it costs is a subscription to whichever assistant is used, and the habit of looking at the page before it goes live. On the pilot program this format came out of11 that help is meant to be local: the workshops are there to leave a few people in the county with a light introduction to AI and the confidence to make a change for a neighbor.

What an editor can break, and how to tell: the words sit between the tags, and the tags are what the browser reads. Delete part of one and the page usually still renders, wrongly — a paragraph swallowed into a heading, a column left half-closed. The checks under Quality assurance catch that at both widths; so does opening the page and looking at it.

Why not WordPress. WordPress15 answers this same question with an editing box, and that box is honest work: it is why somebody with no technical knowledge can change their own page without asking anyone. Its price is a database, an account, a theme, plugins and an update every few weeks, each one a thing that can fail or be broken into. This format takes the other side of the trade: nothing to log in to, nothing to update, and a file that will open in any browser for as long as there are browsers.

A team of one

Put a dot for each person at the corners of a regular polygon and join every pair: each line is a conversation the team has to keep going. Three people make a triangle, three lines; four make a square and its two diagonals, six; ten make forty-five. The count is n(n−1)/2, which grows roughly with the square of the team, so it climbs slowly at first and then steeply — a hockey stick. Fred Brooks gave the same count in The Mythical Man-Month in 1975, as part of the reason that adding people to a late software project makes it later14.

The team this does not need

If the count of lines is the real cost, the test is whether the titles disappear. On a normal web team, a page like this one would be the work of most of these people. Each is a standard job, and each one here is answered by the format rather than by a hire:

product manager         the format asks the questions
UX designer             three columns, fixed order
visual designer         type and color, already set
front-end engineer      one file, four small scripts
back-end engineer       there is no server
database administrator  there is no database
DevOps engineer         publishing is a commit
systems administrator   the domain script
security engineer       nothing versioned to patch
QA engineer             66 checks, and a second reader
release manager         the push is the release
CMS administrator       there is no content system
accessibility engineer  built into the format
SEO specialist          met by construction
analytics engineer      nothing is collected
project manager         nobody to coordinate with

Set those sixteen on the drawing above and you have sixteen more corners and a hundred and twenty lines between them, before anybody has written a word for the page. That is the saving, and it is not that the work goes faster: it is that the conversations never have to happen.

Two of those titles are near-twins and both are real jobs: a product manager decides what goes on the page, a project manager keeps a team in step. The format answers the first. A team of one answers the second.

Two honest limits. This holds for a page of this kind: one subject, a few thousand words, nothing bought or logged into. It does not hold for software in general, and a hospital’s scheduling system needs every one of those people. And three jobs do not go away, because they are the work itself — reading the sources, writing the plain answer, and checking that the two match. The format does not remove that labor. It removes everything standing between it and the page.

The talk is only the visible part of the cost. Brooks’s larger point was conceptual integrity: a design holds together when a single architect, or a very few, decides what goes in and what stays out, and every added mind is another set of assumptions to reconcile. A site this size needs one person: no meetings, no handoffs, no one else’s conventions to learn. Every site in this family is built that way, by one person working with an AI, which drafts and checks while the person decides and publishes. Between people, the count is zero.

One is the fewest it needs, not the most it allows. The site lives in git, which keeps every change with its author, its date and its note, so a second person can take up the same site at any time and read how it got the way it is. If two people change the same lines at once, git stops and shows both versions, and one of them chooses.

The cost of one is the other side of the same coin: with one keeper, what that person knows can leave with them. The format answers that as far as a format can. The page explains its own workings, here in column C; git keeps every change with its note; and nothing in the page’s machinery has a version to go stale while nobody is looking after it. The words can still go out of date, and links still die — the link check above says when one has.

A running record

Optional, and not what most sites will do. Each issue is dated, published the day it is written, and superseded rather than edited — a later issue may correct an earlier one, but the earlier one stays exactly as published.

Files go /index.html for the current issue and /YYYY-MM-DD/index.html for the permanent dated copy, so a link sent today still resolves next year. The version history underneath is a second, independent record of when each word was written.

A site with more than one series gives each a folder of its own, as baconian-ciphers.online does with /biliteral/ and /word-cipher/5. The dated copies are never touched again — not even to add a button the current copy has since gained.

A family of sites

Sites built this way can form a family, wired upward. On an instance that sits under another site in this family, the last thing on the page, below the footer, is a card headed ↑ One level up: the name of the site above it, and one line saying what that site is, in that site’s own words — as at the foot of this page. worland-dog-law.online points to washakie-ai.online11, and washakie-ai.online points to gyro-sites.online12, the class at the top of the family, which carries no such card.

An instance adds what its own subject needs. worland-dog-law.online, three pages long, widens the same search box to cover all three, so a reader on any page finds a section on another6; baconian-ciphers.online, a running record in two series, lists every issue of both on its front page5. What proves itself on one instance is written back into the format — into gyro-sites.online and the template new sites are made from — so the next site starts with it.

Page D — the setup sheet nobody sees

A fourth page exists, unlinked and marked not to be indexed. It is the setup sheet: the header, the picture at the head of the page, the most-recent-change card, the source cards, the one-level-up card, and the last link-check result. Its header knows the two kinds of page — a standing page and a running series — and each block is written out as text to paste into the file.

It is not an admin panel and has no login, because there is nothing behind it to protect. It is a form that produces text you paste into a file. It is kept out of the navigation because it belongs to whoever builds the site, not to whoever reads it. Somebody who came to read about a town’s dog ordinance has no use for a form that writes HTML, and putting it in the menu would only invite the question of what it is for.

Quality assurance

Changes are checked in two different ways before they are handed over for publishing, because each catches what the other misses.

By machine. A script drives a real browser13 over gyro-sites.online and this template, at desktop width (1440 px) and phone width (390 px), in light and dark, and makes 68 checks: no sideways scrolling and no script errors at either width; the most-recent-change card, which must open the passage it names; numerals, which must reach their cards and come back through the Back button, from column A and from column C; the Top button; Phone view, shown to every reader on gyro-sites.online and hidden here until the address carries ?dev; what printing leaves out, and that every answer opens for print and folds again after; all three columns with JavaScript off, on a phone and on a desktop; and every block Page D writes. A change is not handed over until all 68 pass. The script is kept with the format’s tools, so the checks can be run again.

By a second reader. A script checks only what someone thought to test. So larger changes are also read, claim by claim, against the code and against the sites the text describes, by a reviewer that had no part in writing them — a second AI agent, given the files and told to be skeptical. Its first reading of the 21 September update found seventeen problems, several in older text: the page said folded answers printed, and they did not; it said lines were capped near 62 characters, and on a tablet they ran past a hundred; it listed as one of this page’s failures something that could not be made to happen here. Each was fixed or reworded; a second reading confirmed the fixes and caught three more, since fixed.

The links, by hand. Whether each source is still there is checked separately, as Link health, done honestly describes: every source is fetched, and links.json records the date.

The principle. A test checks what a page does; only a reader can check what it says. This page claims that every claim on it is demonstrated by the page itself, and the second reading is how that claim is kept honest.
