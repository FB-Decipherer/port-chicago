What this is

On the night of 17 July 1944 the Port Chicago Naval Magazine, on the shore of Suisun Bay in California, blew up and killed about 320 men. Peter Vogel spent some thirty years arguing that it was no accident. His book, The Last Wave from Port Chicago, is being prepared in a 2026 edition.
Around the year 2000 a brief film of the explosion itself could be watched on Vogel’s website. No copy has been found since. This page sets out what is known about it, where each piece comes from, and what is still open.

A  Plain answers
What is this site?

The home of the 2026 edition of The Last Wave from Port Chicago, and of the search for a lost film of the 1944 explosion. The book is Peter Vogel’s research, which I assembled into a Kindle edition in 2025 and am now preparing again, with a new introduction and corrections.

Every statement here carries a small numeral that opens its source in the middle column, and anything not yet settled is said to be unsettled. The detail, and the list of checks made against the published record, is in column C.

What happened at Port Chicago?

On 17 July 1944, at about 10:18 at night, the Port Chicago Naval Magazine blew up while two cargo ships were being loaded with munitions. About 320 men were killed instantly, nearly two-thirds of them African American sailors of the loading crews; the dead also included merchant seamen and the Navy gun crews aboard the ships, a Marine sentry and civilian employees1.

The Liberty ship E.A. Bryan, at the center of the blast, was destroyed outright. The Victory ship Quinault Victory was lifted out of the water and came down upside down about 500 feet away3. The Navy photographed the damage the next day, and those photographs are public4.

The town of Port Chicago, named in 1931 after the city in Illinois, was later bought by the federal government and demolished; a Port Chicago Highway is still on the map2.

Who was Peter Vogel?

The researcher who spent some thirty years arguing that the explosion was not an accident. His article “The Last Wave from Port Chicago” appeared in the journal The Black Scholar in 19826, and the radio host Dave Emory interviewed him in 1999, 2004 and 2007789.

Around 2001 he put a book of the same name on his website, sixteen chapters and four appendices, 429 pages in all, as PDF files. It looked ready for the printer but was never published as a book. He closed his website in December 2018, and nothing has been heard from him publicly since.

What is the Gadget document?

The document Vogel’s whole project started from: “History of 10,000 Ton Gadget”, a one-page table whose last row reads “Ball of fire mushroom out at 18,000 ft in typical Port Chicago fashion”. Its eleven numbered rows follow an explosion from detonation to the damage area, with columns of times, distances, pressures and temperatures.

Vogel found it in 1980, in an envelope at the bottom of a cardboard box of old photographic papers he bought for 25 cents at the rummage sale of a Santa Fe church, and kept the receipt8. The box had belonged to Paul Masters, a photo technician at wartime Los Alamos. At wartime Los Alamos, “gadget” was the word used in place of “bomb”5. Vogel called it the most detailed mathematical model of an atom bomb explosion the public was ever likely to see, and then left it out of his own book.

The 2026 edition prints it in full on the page after the new introduction.

What is the lost film?

A brief clip of the explosion itself, at night, as it happened. I watched it on Vogel’s website around 2000 and downloaded a copy around 2001. No copy has been found since: not among the research files that came with Vogel’s book, and not in any archive searched so far.

The Navy is said to have denied the film’s authenticity; where and when is still being traced, because a denial of authenticity means somebody was shown something. A 2007 description of one of Emory’s interviews mentions “a film, purporting to be a simulation of the Port Chicago explosion”9. Whether that is the same film is not yet known.

Obtaining a copy of the film would powerfully refute the untruth that the explosion was an accident.

What would the film prove, and what would it not?

It would prove that a film of the explosion existed. On its own it would not prove what kind of explosion it was.

Two questions are kept apart on this page. The first is a question of record: did the film exist, who held it, and where did it go? The second is a question of interpretation: was the explosion nuclear? The search for the film serves the first. A blinding flash and a rising column would be seen either way. What could bear on the second is measurement: the film’s frame rate and the camera’s distance, the growth of the fireball, and the two shocks about seven seconds apart that the seismograph recorded.

A third question sits between them: was the explosion anticipated? A clip of the blast itself, at night, as it happened, means a camera was already running and pointed at the pier. If it was there to record the explosion, the explosion was expected, and it was no accident. The answer a skeptic will give is that the Navy may have been filming the loading routinely; Harry V. Martin raised that possibility himself in 1990. The film’s vantage point, the time it starts, and whether other loading footage exists from the same camera would settle which.

What changes in the 2026 edition?

A new introduction, the Gadget document printed in full, and corrections to the 2025 text. The corrections found so far:

  • the time of the explosion: 10:17 in the text, 22:18:47 Pacific War Time by the seismograph;
  • the date of the explosion, given once as 17 October: it was 17 July;
  • the year I saw the film (2000) against the year I downloaded it (2001);
  • the dead were not all Navy men: merchant seamen and civilian employees died too1;
  • a statement that Dave Emory’s 1999 broadcasts mention the film, which they do not.

Two questions, kept apart

The question of record, whether the film existed and where it went, is answered by documents: catalog entries, transfer records, correspondence, captured copies of web pages. The question of interpretation, whether the explosion was nuclear, is answered, if at all, by measurement. They are kept apart because evidence for the first is easily read as evidence for the second, and because an archivist asked an inventory question gives an inventory answer.

The search for the film

A 1944 Navy film would have been shot on cellulose nitrate stock, which is flammable and decays; archives keep nitrate in separate vaults, with their own inventories and handling records, so a nitrate original, or its transfer to safety film or videotape, leaves a paper trail. The places being asked are the National Archives’ Motion Picture Branch, across record groups 428, 24, 74, 77 and 156, and the Naval History and Heritage Command. The questions are inventory questions only: what is held, what was photographed on the night of 17 July as distinct from the morning after, and what was copied for whom in the 1990s.

Vogel’s own sites are being searched through the Wayback Machine’s capture index, for the film’s file name, its caption and its source credit rather than the film itself. A video streamed in 2000 was usually reached through a small pointer file, and the pointer, if captured, names the stream.
